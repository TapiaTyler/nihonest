# Employment Language Credentials Discovery

Prepared September 13, 2026. The editor approved this Iteration 14A scope on September 13, 2026.

## The guide needs a layered answer

“How much Japanese do I need to work in Japan?” does not have one reliable score answer. The implementation should teach readers to separate five questions:

1. What speaking, listening, reading, writing, and interaction does the actual job require?
2. What has the employer made required, preferred, or advantageous in its selection process?
3. Does the residence route or designated program expressly require language evidence?
4. Does a professional licence, examination, or sector rule impose its own condition?
5. Does the offered certificate actually measure the ability the recipient needs?

That framework supports engineers, designers, teachers, healthcare professionals, service workers, founders, and employers without pretending that a job title decides the answer.

## JLPT is useful evidence with defined limits

The Japanese-Language Proficiency Test certifies non-native speakers across N5 through N1. Its official level descriptions focus on language knowledge, reading, and listening. The JLPT FAQ expressly says that the test does not currently include conversation or composition. The CEFR reference indication added to score reports from the December 2025 test remains score-based and does not add speaking, writing, production, or interaction to the tested skills.[^1][^2]

This creates two important editorial requirements:

- passing N2 or N1 must not be presented as proof of professional speaking, writing, negotiation, customer service, or field vocabulary; and
- an employer can still use JLPT as one screening signal while assessing actual workplace communication through interviews, work samples, or other evidence.

The official certificate does not expire. A company, school, or institution may nevertheless set its own time limit for results. Nihonest should tell users to ask whether the recipient requires a particular test date rather than saying that an old certificate will always be accepted.[^1]

## JFT-Basic answers a different question

JFT-Basic measures Japanese needed for communication in everyday situations by people mainly coming to Japan for work. Its sections address script and vocabulary, conversation and expression, listening, and reading within its A1–A2-oriented framework. It is used as evidence in named immigration procedures, including Specified Skilled Worker (i), but should not be marketed as proof of advanced office, technical, clinical, or management communication.[^3]

For Specified Skilled Worker (i), the current general route uses an accepted Japanese test plus the relevant field skill test unless an exemption applies. ISA currently identifies higher N3 thresholds for taxi or bus driving and train-operation work, and an additional Nursing Care Japanese Language Evaluation Test for nursing-care work. The implementation must use the current field source rather than freezing “N4 for every SSW job” into search metadata.[^4]

## Some immigration and licensing frameworks name language evidence

Named exceptions are the reason the canonical article needs careful links instead of the slogan “Japanese is only an employer preference.” Examples include:

- Designated Activities No. 46 for eligible Japanese-university graduates, which has its own advanced-Japanese framework;[^5]
- Highly Skilled Professional point calculations, which can recognize JLPT or BJT results while still requiring the underlying authorized activity;[^6]
- the revised Business Manager framework, whose B2-oriented Japanese-capacity condition is not necessarily an applicant-held JLPT certificate;[^7]
- foreign-trained nurse examination-eligibility recognition, where MHLW identifies JLPT N1 in the stated education circumstances alongside separate examination and licensing requirements;[^8] and
- Specified Skilled Worker field tests and language evidence.[^4]

These should appear as exceptions and handoffs. The new guide should not reproduce every route's full criteria or let a language score stand in for the status, contract, education, experience, remuneration, licence, or employer analysis.

## Job families need task examples, not invented score tables

The guide should help a reader inspect the communication environment:

- software and engineering: specifications, stand-ups, code review, incident response, documentation, clients, and safety;
- design and creative work: briefs, critique, revisions, production handoffs, rights and contracts, and client presentations;
- research and academia: lab or department communication, papers, teaching, grant administration, ethics, and conferences;
- office, sales, and consulting: email, meetings, proposals, negotiation, reporting, and customer relationships;
- hospitality and customer service: live requests, complaints, payments, accessibility, emergencies, and shift handoffs;
- skilled and safety-sensitive work: instructions, hazards, equipment, records, supervisors, and field-specific assessments;
- medical and care work: licensing, examinations, clinical communication, consent, records, emergencies, and patient safety; and
- English teaching: institution, designated language, teaching duties, learner communication, program rules, and Japanese-language administrative work.

Nihonest should not assign its own N-level to those tasks. It should show readers what to ask and explain why two employers using the same job title can reasonably publish different requirements.

## “Native English” is not one legal category

Teaching guidance needs to distinguish employer wording from immigration evidence. ISA's Instructor materials ask a foreign-language teacher for evidence of twelve or more years of education through the language in the applicable context. That is not the same as a citizenship rule, a universal native-speaker rule, a TEFL requirement, or a JLPT requirement.[^9]

The JET Programme illustrates another boundary. Its current general criteria concern ability in the designated language. Prior Japanese is not a universal ALT eligibility requirement, while CIR applicants need functional Japanese described as approximately JLPT N1 or N2. Teaching credentials and experience can receive additional consideration without being universal ALT eligibility requirements. These are JET program rules, not rules for every ALT, direct-hire school, dispatch company, eikaiwa, international school, or university.[^10]

The article should help applicants translate “native,” “native-level,” “fluent,” and “business English” into concrete questions: which skills, which evidence, which duties, and whether the wording is mandatory or preferred. Where English test evidence matters, it should identify the module. TOEIC Speaking and Writing, for example, is a separate assessment from Listening and Reading.[^11]

## Employer guidance should remain bounded

MHLW's fair-recruitment guidance asks employers to base selection on the aptitude and ability needed to perform the job and to avoid collecting unrelated personal information.[^12] A concise employer section can turn that into useful practice:

- define the communication tasks before selecting a certificate threshold;
- distinguish required, preferred, and trainable abilities;
- assess the skill actually used by the role;
- state accepted alternatives and certificate-recency policies; and
- avoid using nationality or unrelated background as a substitute for language ability.

Nihonest should not adjudicate whether an individual advertisement is discriminatory. It can identify the official principle and direct readers to appropriate employment support.

## Recommended Iteration 14B scope

Create `understanding-language-requirements-and-certifications-for-jobs-in-japan` as the canonical guide. Add it to Working in Japan and cross-link it from Professional work, but do not make it a mandatory journey step for every worker.

The first implementation should also:

1. create glossary entries for JLPT, JFT-Basic, and BJT because each has a distinct scope and BJT has named immigration uses;
2. add focused links and corrections to the teaching comparison, Instructor, Engineer/Specialist in Humanities/International Services, Specified Skilled Worker, Medical Services, Business Manager, Highly Skilled Professional, Designated Activities, and work-status chooser;
3. add FAQs and search phrases for ordinary questions and professions; and
4. retain TOEIC as an article example unless later search evidence justifies a dedicated glossary entry.

## Approved scope decisions

The editor approved the recommended answers to the unresolved questions:

- create BJT as a glossary term now, but keep TOEIC in contextual prose;
- include a concise employer-facing section in the canonical guide rather than creating another article; and
- place the guide in Working in Japan and cross-link it from Professional work without adding a mandatory journey step.
