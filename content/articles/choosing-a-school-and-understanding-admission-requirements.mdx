## Start with the result you need

Decide whether you need Japanese-language preparation, an undergraduate degree, graduate research, professional training, exchange credit, non-degree study, or a short experience. Then compare the exact course—not only the school's name. Its teaching language, qualification, schedule, workload, and immigration suitability must fit the same plan.

School admission and immigration approval are separate. A school can admit you without guaranteeing that the Immigration Services Agency will issue a Certificate of Eligibility ([在留資格認定証明書](/glossary/certificate-of-eligibility), **zairyū shikaku nintei shōmeisho**), that a diplomatic mission will issue a visa, or that landing will be permitted.

## Compare the main education routes

### Japanese-language institutions

Check whether the Japanese-language institution ([日本語教育機関](/glossary/nihongo-kyoiku-kikan), **nihongo kyōiku kikan**) and course appear in the current official system applicable to students using Student status ([留学](/glossary/ryugaku), **ryūgaku**). Japan is transitioning to MEXT certification for Japanese-language institutions. Existing institutions under the prior ISA framework may continue during the transition through March 31, 2029, but do not rely on advertising such as “visa school.” Verify the institution through the current official lists.

Compare class level and size, course intensity, intake dates, JLPT or EJU preparation, progression and career support, graduate outcomes, attendance rules, counseling, housing, and refund terms.

### Universities and junior colleges

A university ([大学](/glossary/daigaku), **daigaku**) normally offers undergraduate degree programs, while a junior college ([短期大学](/glossary/tanki-daigaku), **tanki daigaku**) normally offers shorter associate-degree programs. Admission commonly considers prior education, examinations, language, and program-specific evidence. Twelve years of education is a common route, but preparatory courses, recognized equivalents, and individual eligibility assessment can matter. Japanese-taught and English-taught programs have different language expectations; an English-taught program does not remove the financial or immigration review.

### Graduate schools

A graduate school ([大学院](/glossary/daigakuin), **daigakuin**) can offer master's, doctoral, professional-degree, and non-degree research arrangements. Confirm degree equivalence, prior years of education, research fit, supervisor-contact rules, proposal requirements, examinations, and whether the course is a regular degree or a non-degree research arrangement. Sixteen years of education is common for master's admission, but it is not an exceptionless rule. Ask the institution to assess an unusual education history before assuming you qualify or do not qualify.

### Professional training colleges

A professional training college, commonly called a **senmon gakkō** ([専門学校](/glossary/senmon-gakko)), provides an approved postsecondary specialized-training course. Verify the formal institution and course category, the qualification awarded, and whether the curriculum supports your career plan. These programs are generally taught in Japanese. Official guidance lists several ways a foreign applicant may demonstrate Japanese ability; the accepted route and score must be checked for the actual program rather than copied from another school.

### Exchange, audit, research, and short programs

For exchange study ([交換留学](/glossary/kokan-ryugaku), **kōkan ryūgaku**), check who awards or transfers credit, where tuition is paid, and what the home-school agreement actually covers. For audit, research, and other short programs, confirm which immigration basis the host expects. “Short-term program” can mean anything from a few weeks to a full academic year and does not itself mean Temporary Visitor ([短期滞在](/glossary/tanki-taizai), **tanki taizai**).

## Verify admission and immigration separately

Ask the school for its current requirements for:

- prior education, transcripts, graduation evidence, and translations;
- examinations, portfolios, research proposals, or interviews;
- Japanese or English language evidence;
- application, admission, tuition, materials, and other fees;
- proof that you or an expense supporter can fund the course and living costs;
- document issue dates, originals, certification, and return of originals;
- admission, COE, visa, housing, orientation, and enrollment deadlines; and
- deferral, withdrawal, refusal, and refund rules.

Do not use one school's checklist as a national checklist. Institution type, course, nationality, education history, and application context can change what is requested.

## Compare the full cost and support

Budget beyond tuition: application and admission charges, examinations, materials, insurance, housing deposits, travel, transport, food, utilities, phone service, and an emergency reserve. Do not make uncertain part-time income the foundation of the plan; Student status does not itself permit work.

Ask what the school actually provides for housing, disability access, mental health, harassment, emergencies, academic difficulty, immigration referrals, career planning, and multilingual communication. Obtain important promises and refund conditions in writing.

> **Before paying:** Verify the institution and exact course through an official source, read the school's current contract and refund terms, and identify what happens if admission is deferred or the COE or visa is not issued.

## Watch for warning signs

Pause if an agent or school promises government approval, asks you to misstate study purpose or finances, will not identify fees, refuses to provide refund terms, wants original documents without a return process, or says attendance and work limits do not matter. The school can support an application; it cannot override ISA, a diplomatic mission, or the landing examination.

After choosing a course, use the Student COE guide for long-term study or the short-term guide to confirm whether a visitor or another route fits the actual program.
